Part I · Mechanical Ventilation & Respiratory Failure

1 Neuromuscular Blockers in Early ARDS — ACURASYS

Papazian et al. · NEJM 2010 · 20 French ICUs, 2006–2008

✔ Signal of benefit (later refuted)

Question: Does 48 h of cisatracurium early in severe ARDS improve survival?

Design

  • 340 patients · severe ARDS PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150 · on lung-protective ventilation
  • Cisatracurium 15 mg bolus → 37.5 mg/h × 48 h vs placebo
  • Deep sedation (Ramsay 6) in both arms

Key numbers

OutcomeCisatracuriumPlacebo
90-day mortality (crude)31.6%40.7% (P=0.08)
90-day mortality (adjusted)HR 0.68 (0.48–0.98), P=0.04
28-day mortality23.7%33.3% (P=0.05)
Ventilator-free days (90 d)53.144.6 (P=0.03)
Organ failure–free days (28 d)15.812.2 (P=0.01)
ICU-acquired paresis (day 28)70.8% free67.5% free (NS)

Pearls

  • Benefit confined to PaO₂/FiO₂ < 120 mmHg
  • Pneumothorax less common with cisatracurium (4% vs 11.7%)
  • Underpowered — placebo mortality lower than expected (authors said 885 needed)
ROSE trial (NEJM 2019): 1006 patients, stopped for futility — 90-day mortality 42.5% vs 42.8% (P=0.93). Light sedation control, higher PEEP, more prone (ROSE 16% vs ACURASYS 50%). No routine paralysis.

2 Low Tidal Volume Ventilation — ARDSNet

ARDS Network · NEJM 2000 · 10 US university centres, 1996–1999

✔ Practice-changing benefit

Question: Does 6 mL/kg PBW reduce mortality vs 12 mL/kg in ARDS?

Design

  • 861 patients · ARDS (PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤ 300, bilateral infiltrates, no LA hypertension)
  • Low TV 6 mL/kg (Pplat ≤ 30) vs traditional 12 mL/kg (Pplat 45–50)
  • Stopped at 4th planned interim analysis

Key numbers

Outcome6 mL/kg12 mL/kg
Death before discharge / before unassisted breathing31.0%39.8% (P=0.007)
Breathing without assistance by day 2865.7%55.0% (P<0.001)
Ventilator-free days (1–28)12 ± 1110 ± 11 (P=0.007)
Organ failure–free days15 ± 1112 ± 11 (P=0.006)
Barotrauma10%11% (NS)

Pearls

  • Mean TV days 1–3: 6.2 vs 11.8 mL/kg · Pplat 25 vs 33 cmH₂O
  • IL-6 fell more in low-TV group → less biotrauma
  • Criticism: control arm received higher TV/Pplat than usual practice
Amato 2015 (mediation analysis of 3562 ARDS patients): Driving pressure (Pplat − PEEP) is the strongest predictor of survival — low TV helps only if it lowers driving pressure.

3 Prone Ventilation — PROSEVA

Guérin et al. · NEJM 2013 · 26 French + 1 Spanish ICU, 2008–2011

✔ Mortality benefit

Question: Does prolonged prone positioning improve survival in severe ARDS?

Design

  • 466 patients · ARDS with PaO₂/FiO₂ < 150, FiO₂ > 0.6, PEEP ≥ 5
  • Prone ≥ 16 consecutive hours/day vs supine semi-recumbent
  • 12–24 h stabilisation before randomisation · centres experienced in proning

Key numbers

OutcomeProneSupine
28-day mortality16%32.8% (P<0.001)
90-day mortality23.6%41% (P<0.001)
Successful extubation at 90 d80.5%65%
Ventilator-free days (28 d)14 ± 910 ± 10 (P<0.001)
Cardiac arrest1631

Pearls

  • Median prone duration achieved: 17 ± 3 h per session
  • Earlier Gattinoni 2001 trial: only ~6 h/day, no mortality benefit → dose matters
  • Only ~1/3 of screened patients randomised (highly selected)
  • Baseline imbalance: lower SOFA / more vasopressors in supine arm — benefit persisted after adjustment

4 Early vs Late Tracheostomy — TracMan

Young et al. · JAMA 2013 · 82 UK hospitals, 2004–2008

— No difference

Question: Does tracheostomy within 4 days improve outcomes vs waiting to day 10?

Design

  • 899 randomised (target 1692 — recruitment failed → underpowered)
  • Early = tracheostomy ≤ 4 days of ICU admission
    Late = day 10 or later if still indicated
  • 88.7% percutaneous bedside technique

Key numbers

OutcomeEarlyLate
30-day mortality30.8%31.5% (P=0.89)
ICU mortality29.7%29.7%
Hospital mortality39.6%41.3% (P=0.63)
2-year mortality51%53.7% (P=0.42)
Sedation days (30-day survivors)5 (3–9)8 (4–12)

Pearls

  • 53.7% of the late group never needed a tracheostomy → early strategy risks unnecessary procedures
  • Bleeding: 2.6% early vs 3.9% late
  • No validated tool exists to predict need for prolonged ventilation

5 High-Frequency Oscillation — OSCILLATE

Ferguson et al. · NEJM 2013 · 39 ICUs, 5 countries

✘ Harm — stopped early

Question: Does early HFOV reduce mortality in moderate–severe ARDS?

Design

  • Planned 1200; stopped at 548 for safety (mortality signal)
  • HFOV with recruitment manoeuvre (40 cmH₂O × 40 s), mPaw 30 cmH₂O
    vs conventional pressure-controlled, TV 6 mL/kg, high PEEP
  • SensorMedics 3100B oscillator

Key numbers

OutcomeHFOVConventional
Hospital mortality47%35% (P=0.005)
ICU mortality45%31% (P=0.001)
28-day mortality40%29% (P=0.004)
Vasoactive drug use91%84% (P=0.01)

Pearls

  • Vasopressor use rose within 4 h of starting HFOV → haemodynamic compromise is the proposed mechanism of harm
  • Barotrauma more common with HFOV (NS)
  • 12% of conventional arm crossed over to HFOV

6 High-Frequency Oscillation — OSCAR

Young et al. · NEJM 2013 (same issue as OSCILLATE) · 27 UK ICUs, 2007–2012

— No difference

Question: Same question as OSCILLATE — HFOV vs conventional ventilation in ARDS.

Design

  • 795 randomised (target 1006) · PaO₂/FiO₂ ≤ 200, PEEP ≥ 5
  • HFOV via Novalung R100 (different device from OSCILLATE)
  • Conventional arm: local practice, TV 6–8 mL/kg, ARDSNet PEEP–FiO₂ table

Key numbers

OutcomeHFOVConventional
30-day mortality41.7%41.1% (P=0.85)
ICU mortality44.1%42.1% (P=0.57)
Hospital mortality50.1%48.4% (P=0.62)
ICU stay17.6 d16.1 d (P=0.18)
Hospital stay33.9 d33.1 d (P=0.79)

Pearls

  • No benefit and no clear harm — but no reason to use HFOV
  • More neuromuscular blockade used in HFOV arm
  • Meta-analysis (Maitra, 7 RCTs, 1759 pts): no mortality benefit; trend to longer ventilation with HFOV

7 PEEP Setting in ARDS — ExPress

Mercat et al. · JAMA 2008 · 37 French ICUs, 2002–2005

— No mortality difference

Question: Increased recruitment (high PEEP) vs minimal distension (low PEEP) in ALI/ARDS?

Design

  • 767 analysed · PaO₂:FiO₂ ≤ 300, bilateral infiltrates
  • Increased recruitment: PEEP titrated to keep Pplat 28–30 cmH₂O (max PEEP)
    Minimal distension: total PEEP 5–9 cmH₂O
  • Both: volume-assist control, TV 6 mL/kg PBW
  • Stopped for futility

Key numbers

OutcomeIncreased recruitmentMinimal distension
28-day mortality27.8%31.2% (P=0.31)
Hospital mortality35.4%39.0% (P=0.30)
60-day mortality35.9%39.5% (P=0.31)
Ventilator-free days (28 d)7 (0–19)3 (0–17) (P=0.04)
Organ failure–free days6 (0–18)2 (0–16) (P=0.04)
Rescue therapy (prone/iNO)18.7%34.6% (P<0.001)
Pneumothorax6.8%5.8% (NS)

Pearls

  • Higher PEEP → better oxygenation, compliance, more ventilator- and organ-failure-free days, fewer rescue therapies — but no survival benefit
  • Unblinded; no formal adherence monitoring
After ExPress: PHARLAP (open lung) — no difference. ART trial — recruitment + PEEP titration to best compliance → higher 28-day mortality. Kacmarek open-lung — no mortality difference. Take-home: empirically high PEEP does not help; individualise.

8 Methods of Weaning from Mechanical Ventilation

Esteban et al. · NEJM 1995 · 14 Spanish medical–surgical ICUs, 1992–1993

✔ Once-daily SBT wins

Question: Which weaning method gets difficult-to-wean patients off the ventilator fastest?

Design

  • 546 ventilated patients; 416 passed the initial 2-h T-piece SBT and were extubated
  • 130 "difficult to wean" randomised to 4 methods
  • IMV (rate ↓), PSV (pressure ↓), intermittent SBT (≥2/day), once-daily SBT
  • Success = extubation within 14 days with no reintubation in 48 h

Time to extubation (median)

MethodMedian days (IQR)
Once-daily SBT3 (1–6)
Intermittent SBT (≥2/day)3 (2–6)
Pressure support ventilation4 (2–12)
Intermittent mandatory ventilation5 (3–11)

Relative rate of successful weaning

ComparisonRelative rate (95% CI)
Once-daily SBT vs IMV2.83 (1.36–5.89)
Once-daily SBT vs PSV2.05 (1.04–4.04)
Once-daily SBT vs intermittent SBT1.24 (0.64–2.41) — NS

Pearls

  • Once-daily SBT ≈ 3× faster than IMV, 2× faster than PSV
  • Extubation and reintubation rates similar across all four arms
  • Small groups (29–37 per arm); uneven COPD distribution
Subira: 30-min PSV SBT (PEEP 0, PS 8) vs 2-h T-piece → PSV had more successful extubation (82.3% vs 74.0%) and lower hospital/90-day mortality. Current guidelines do not mandate a specific SBT technique.

9 Non-Invasive Ventilation in Acute COPD Exacerbation

Brochard et al. · NEJM 1995 · 5 hospitals (Paris ×3, Italy, Spain), 1990–1991

✔ Landmark benefit

Question: Does NIV (face mask pressure support) reduce intubation and mortality in acute hypercapnic COPD exacerbation?

Design

  • 85 patients randomised (42 standard care, 43 NIV + standard care)
  • Known/likely COPD · dyspnoea < 2 weeks · ≥2 of: RR > 30, PaO₂ < 45 mmHg, pH < 7.35
  • NIV: face mask, inspiratory pressure support 20 cmH₂O, no PEEP, ≥ 6 h/day
  • Duration of NIV: 4 ± 4 days

Key numbers

OutcomeNIVStandard care
Endotracheal intubation26%74% (P<0.001)
In-hospital mortality9% (4/43)29% (12/42) (P=0.02)
Hospital stay23 ± 17 d35 ± 33 d (P=0.02)
Complications16%48% (P=0.001)

Pearls

  • Within 1 h: NIV improved encephalopathy score, RR, PaO₂ and pH; standard care worsened
  • Intubation was the main factor driving the mortality difference
  • Only 31% of admitted COPD patients were enrolled → highly selective
  • Fragility index = 1 — one extra death in NIV arm would have nullified the mortality result
Subsequent evidence: Cochrane meta-analysis (12 RCTs, 854 pts) — NIV reduced mortality by 46% and intubation by 65%; benefit in both mild (pH 7.30–7.35) and severe (pH < 7.30) acidosis. NIV is now first-line in acute hypercapnic COPD exacerbation.

Part II · Fluid Management

10 Albumin vs Normal Saline — SAFE

Finfer et al. · NEJM 2004 · 16 ANZ ICUs, 6997 patients

— No overall difference

Question: Is 4% albumin safer/efficacious than 0.9% saline for ICU fluid resuscitation?

Design

  • 6997 ICU patients needing fluid resuscitation · double-blind
  • 4% albumin vs 0.9% saline, 500 mL masked bottles
  • Primary: all-cause mortality at 28 days

Key numbers

OutcomeAlbuminSaline
28-day mortality20.9%21.1% (RR 0.99)
New organ failureNo difference
ICU / hospital LOSNo difference
MV / RRT durationNo difference

Subgroups

  • Trauma: albumin harm signal — RR 1.36 vs non-trauma 0.96 (P=0.04)
  • Severe sepsis: trend to benefit — RR 0.87 vs 1.05 (P=0.06)
SAFE-TBI (24 months): albumin mortality 33.2% vs 29.4% saline; RR 1.63 (1.17–2.26), P=0.003. Avoid albumin in traumatic brain injury. Overall: albumin is safe but offers no survival advantage; avoid in TBI.

11 Albumin in Severe Sepsis — ALBIOS

Caironi et al. · NEJM 2014 · 100 Italian ICUs, 1818 patients

— No overall survival benefit

Question: Does 20% albumin + crystalloid improve outcomes in severe sepsis/septic shock?

Design

  • Severe sepsis within 24 h · 20% albumin to keep serum albumin ≥30 g/L + crystalloid vs crystalloid alone
  • Primary: 28-day mortality

Key numbers

OutcomeAlbuminCrystalloid
28-day mortality31.8%32.0% (RR 1.00, NS)
90-day mortality41.1%43.6% (RR 0.94, NS)
Vasopressor cessation3 days4 days (P=0.007)

Post hoc

  • Septic shock subgroup: 90-day mortality 43.6% vs 49.9%, RR 0.87 (P=0.03) — hypothesis-generating
  • No benefit in severe sepsis without shock
Take-home: Albumin did not improve survival overall. It improved haemodynamics and shortened vasopressor use. Possible benefit in septic shock remains unproven.

16 Conservative vs Liberal Fluid in ARDS — FACTT

ARDS Network · NEJM 2006 · 20 North American centres, 1000 patients

✔ Lung/ICU benefit, no mortality difference

Question: Does a conservative fluid strategy improve outcomes in acute lung injury/ARDS?

Design

  • 1000 patients with ALI/ARDS · 2×2 factorial: fluid strategy × catheter type
  • Conservative: CVP <4 mmHg or PAOP <8 mmHg, furosemide if needed
    Liberal: CVP 10–14 mmHg or PAOP 14–18 mmHg
  • Primary: 60-day in-hospital mortality

Key numbers

OutcomeConservativeLiberal
60-day mortality25.5%28.4% (P=0.30)
Ventilator-free days (28 d)14.6 ± 0.512.1 ± 0.5 (P<0.001)
ICU-free days (1–28)13.4 ± 0.411.2 ± 0.4 (P<0.001)
7-day fluid balance136 ± 491 mL6992 ± 502 mL (P<0.001)

Pearls

  • No difference in RRT requirement or organ failure–free days
  • Electrolyte abnormalities (hypokalaemia, metabolic alkalosis) more common with conservative strategy, but no arrhythmias
  • PAC vs CVC: no outcome difference; PAC had twice the catheter-related complications
Take-home: Conservative fluid strategy improves lung function, shortens ventilation and ICU stay, without increasing organ failure. Avoid fluid overload once shock has resolved.

Part III · Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation

17 Compression-Only CPR in Out-of-Hospital Cardiac Arrest — DART

Rea et al. · NEJM 2010 · 3 EMS systems (Washington State ×2, London Ambulance Service), 2004–2008

✔ Compression-only at least as good

Question: For dispatcher-assisted bystander CPR, is compression-only as effective as compressions + rescue breathing?

Design

  • Adult (>18 y) out-of-hospital cardiac arrest · dispatcher-assisted CPR · randomised in blocks of 10, stratified by dispatch centre
  • Compression-only: 50 consecutive compressions → check for signs of life → continue
  • Standard CPR: 15 compressions : 2 rescue breaths
  • Primary outcome: survival to hospital discharge
  • Excluded: trauma, drowning, asphyxiation, DNR orders, arrests already receiving CPR

Population

  • 5525 randomisation envelopes opened → 1941 met inclusion criteria (981 compression-only vs 960 conventional)
  • Cardiac cause ~70% · witnessed <50% · shockable rhythm ~1/3
  • Mean dispatch-to-EMS arrival time: 6.5 min

Key numbers

OutcomeCompression-onlyCompressions + breaths
Survival to hospital discharge12.5% (122/978)11.0% (105/956) (P=0.31)
ROSC at end of EMS care38.5%31.3% (P=0.005)
Survival with favourable neuro outcome (CPC 1–2)14.4%11.5% (P=0.13)
Survival — cardiac cause subgroup15.5%12.3% (P=0.09)
Survival — shockable rhythm subgroup31.9%25.7% (P=0.09)
Survival with good neuro — cardiac cause18.9%13.5% (P=0.03)

Pearls

  • Study powered for a 3.5% absolute difference — overall primary outcome was underpowered for neurological subgroup
  • Rescue breathing interrupts compressions and raises intrathoracic pressure → ↓ venous return, ↓ coronary perfusion
  • Lay public reluctant to perform mouth-to-mouth (infection fear)
  • 15:2 ratio used (AHA now recommends 30:2) — a limitation
  • Applicable to untrained bystanders, not trained EMS personnel
Other evidence:
• Svensson (Swedish EMS, same NEJM issue): 1276 patients, 30-day survival 8.7% vs 7.0% (P=0.29) — no difference.
• Cabrini meta-analysis (3 RCTs, 3737 pts): survival to discharge 11.5% vs 9.4%, P=0.04 favouring compression-only.
• Hüpfl meta-analysis: dispatcher-assisted compression-only → 22% improvement in survival vs standard CPR.
Take-home: For lay bystanders in out-of-hospital cardiac arrest, compression-only CPR is at least as effective as standard CPR and may be superior — especially for cardiac-cause and shockable-rhythm arrests. Trained EMS must still use standard CPR. Ventilation remains essential in primary respiratory causes (drowning, asphyxia).

Part IV · Neurosciences

18 ICP Monitoring in TBI — BEST-TRIP

Chesnut et al. · NEJM 2012 · 6 sites (4 Bolivia, 2 Ecuador), 2008–2012

— No benefit

Question: Does ICP-guided management improve outcomes vs clinical exam + CT in severe TBI?

Design

  • 324 patients · severe TBI · GCS 3–8 (or motor 1–5 if intubated)
  • ICP group: intraparenchymal catheter, target ICP < 20 mmHg
    I-CE group: management guided by exam + serial CT, osmotherapy, brief hyperventilation
  • Primary: composite of 21 parameters (survival, consciousness, function, neuropsych) at 6 months

Key numbers

OutcomeICP groupI-CE group
Composite primary outcome (0–100, higher = better)5653 (P=0.49)
6-month mortality39%41% (OR 1.10)
GOS-E favourable (5–8)44%39%
ICU length of stay12 days9 days (P=0.25)

Pearls

  • Conducted in Latin America where ICP monitoring was not routine → equipoise possible
  • I-CE group received more osmotherapy, hypertonic saline, hyperventilation and barbiturates
  • 10 catheter-related adverse events (malfunction 3%, accidental removal 3%, haemorrhage 1)
Take-home: Does not challenge ICP monitoring itself — challenges the fixed 20 mmHg threshold algorithm. May not apply to centres where ICP monitoring is standard. Suggests exam + CT can be adequate in resource-limited settings.

19 Methylprednisolone in TBI — CRASH

Roberts et al. · Lancet 2004 (2-week) / 2005 (6-month) · 239 hospitals, 49 countries, 1999–2004

✘ Harm

Question: Does early methylprednisolone improve survival in clinically significant head injury?

Design

  • 10,008 adults · GCS ≤ 14 · within 8 h of injury
  • Methylprednisolone: 2 g IV over 1 h, then 0.4 mg/h × 48 h vs placebo
  • Randomised using the "uncertainty principle"
  • Target 20,000 patients; stopped early at interim analysis

Key numbers

OutcomeMethylprednisolonePlacebo
2-week mortality21% (1052/4985)18% (893/4979) (RR 1.18, P=0.0001)
6-month mortality25.7%22.3% (RR 1.15, P=0.0001)
Death or severe disability (6 m)38.1%36.3% (RR 1.05, P=0.079)

Pearls

  • No increase in infection, seizures, GI bleeding, or neurosurgical intervention
  • Harm mechanism unexplained; hyperglycaemia may have unblinded clinicians
  • Contrasted with NASCIS-2 spinal cord data that spurred enthusiasm
Take-home: Corticosteroids are contraindicated in traumatic brain injury, regardless of severity. Landmark example of a plausible therapy causing measurable harm in a large population.

20 Thrombectomy 6–24 h — DAWN

Nogueira et al. · NEJM 2018 · 26 centres (US, Canada, Europe, Australia), 2014–2017

✔ Practice-changing benefit

Question: Does endovascular thrombectomy beyond 6 h improve outcomes when clinical deficit is out of proportion to infarct size?

Design

  • 206 patients · ICA or proximal MCA occlusion · last known well 6–24 h ago
  • Clinical–core mismatch criteria (3 groups):
    A: ≥80 y, core <21 mL, NIHSS ≥10
    B: <80 y, core <31 mL, NIHSS ≥10
    C: <80 y, core 31–50 mL, NIHSS ≥20
  • Mechanical thrombectomy + standard care vs standard care alone
  • Adaptive Bayesian design; stopped at first interim analysis

Key numbers

OutcomeThrombectomyControl
Utility-weighted mRS (0–10, higher better)5.5 ± 3.83.4 ± 3.1 (posterior prob >0.999)
Functional independence at 90 d (mRS 0–2)49%13% (posterior prob >0.999)
Early neurological response48%19% (P<0.001)
Recanalisation at 24 h77%39% (P<0.001)
Infarct volume at 24 h8 mL22 mL (P≤0.001)
Symptomatic ICH6%3% (NS)

Pearls

  • Median time last-known-well → randomisation: 12.2 vs 13.3 h
  • 63% of thrombectomy and 47% of control were wake-up strokes
  • NNT = 2 for reduced disability
  • Benefit independent of age, stroke severity, occlusion site, time to treatment
Take-home: Selection by mismatch between deficit and infarct core (CT/MR perfusion) — not the clock — determines who benefits. Extended the thrombectomy window to 24 h in selected patients.

21 Decompressive Craniectomy in Diffuse TBI — DECRA

Cooper et al. · NEJM 2011 · 15 ICUs (ANZ + Saudi Arabia), 2002–2010

✘ Worse functional outcomes

Question: Does early decompressive craniectomy improve outcomes in severe diffuse TBI with refractory intracranial hypertension?

Design

  • 155 patients · 15–59 y · severe non-penetrating TBI · Marshall class III
  • Refractory ICP: ICP > 20 mmHg for > 15 min in a 1-h period despite tier-1 therapy
  • Bifrontotemporoparietal craniectomy + dura opening vs continued standard care
  • Primary: Extended Glasgow Outcome Scale at 6 months

Key numbers

OutcomeCraniectomyStandard care
Unfavourable outcome (death/vegetative/severe disability)70% (51/73)51% (42/82) (OR 2.21, P=0.02)
Mean ICP after intervention14.4 mmHg19.1 mmHg
Ventilation days (median)1115 (P<0.001)
ICU days (median)1318 (P<0.001)

  • ICP fell dramatically — but functional outcomes were worse
  • Proposed mechanism: brain swelling outside the skull → axonal stretch
  • Bilateral technique may be less favourable than unilateral

24 IV Thrombolysis 3–4.5 h — ECASS III

Hacke et al. · NEJM 2008 · 130 sites, 19 countries, 2003–2007

✔ Extended window

Question: Does alteplase improve outcomes when given 3–4.5 h after ischaemic stroke onset?

Design

  • 821 patients · 18–80 y · stroke within 3–4.5 h · double-blind
  • Alteplase 0.9 mg/kg (max 90 mg; 10% bolus, rest over 60 min) vs placebo
  • Primary: mRS 0–1 (favourable) at day 90

25 Four Treatments for Generalized Convulsive Status Epilepticus — Treiman

Treiman et al. · NEJM 1998 · 16 VA + 6 university hospitals, USA, 1990–1995

✔ Lorazepam best

Question: Which of four IV regimens is most effective for GCSE?

Design

  • 570 enrolled (518 verified GCSE) · double-blind · 4 arms
  • Diazepam 0.15 mg/kg + phenytoin 18 mg/kg
    Lorazepam 0.1 mg/kg
    Phenobarbital 15 mg/kg
    Phenytoin 18 mg/kg alone
  • Success: cessation of clinical + electrical seizures within 20 min, no recurrence 20–60 min

Success rates — verified overt GCSE

DrugSuccess
Lorazepam64.9%
Phenobarbital58.2%
Diazepam + phenytoin55.8%

Key numbers

  • Subtle GCSE success much lower and no difference between arms (P=0.18)
  • Combined overt + subtle: lorazepam 52.2%, phenobarbital 49.2%, diazepam-phenytoin 43.1%, phenytoin 36.8%
  • Subtle GCSE: only 8.8% discharged vs 50.1% in overt; 30-day mortality 64.7% vs 27%
  • Adverse events (hypotension, hypoventilation, arrhythmias): similar across groups
  • ~50% of overt GCSE needed a second agent; response to second drug only 7%
Modern context: Lorazepam remains first-line. IM midazolam is an alternative pre-hospital (RAMPART). Levetiracetam/valproate now used as second-line. Status definition changed to ≥5 min (Neurocritical Care Society 2012).

26 Clipping vs Coiling in Ruptured Aneurysms — ISAT

Molyneux et al. · Lancet 2002 · 43 centres (Europe + N. America), 1994–2002

✔ Coiling better

Question: For ruptured intracranial aneurysms suitable for either, is endovascular coiling or surgical clipping better?

Design

  • 2143 patients · SAH proven by CT/LP · aneurysm confirmed on angiography · suitable for both
  • Neurosurgical clipping vs endovascular platinum coils
  • Minimisation algorithm balancing age, sex, grade, aneurysm location, haemorrhage extent
  • Primary: death or dependency (mRS 3–6) at 1 year
  • Stopped early at planned interim analysis

  • 97.3% anterior circulation · most common: anterior cerebral, ICA, MCA

29 Hydrocortisone–Fludrocortisone in Septic Shock — Annane et al.

Annane et al. · JAMA 2002 · 19 French ICUs, 299 patients

✔ Benefit in non-responders

Question: Do low-dose hydrocortisone + fludrocortisone improve survival in septic shock with relative adrenal insufficiency?

Design

  • 299 patients · septic shock (infection + SIRS + hypotension despite fluids + vasopressors + organ dysfunction)
  • Hydrocortisone 50 mg q6h IV + fludrocortisone 50 µg NG daily × 7 days vs placebo
  • Short corticotrophin test (250 µg tetracosactrin)
    Non-responders: cortisol rise ≤ 9 µg/dL
    Responders: rise > 9 µg/dL
  • Primary: 28-day mortality in non-responders

Key numbers — non-responders (n=229)

OutcomeCorticosteroidPlacebo
28-day mortality53% (60/114)63% (73/115) (OR 0.54, P=0.04)
ICU mortality58%70% (OR 0.50, P=0.02)
Hospital mortality61%72% (OR 0.53, P=0.04)

Responders

  • No mortality difference — placebo 53% vs corticosteroid 61% (P=0.96)
  • Overall (responders + non-responders): no significant difference in 28-day mortality (55% vs 61%)

Pearls

  • Vasopressor cessation earlier in non-responders: 10 vs 7 days; at day 28, 57% vs 40% off vasopressors

33 Corticosteroids in Persistent ARDS — LaSRS

Steinberg et al. · NEJM 2006 · 25 US hospitals (NHLBI ARDS Network), 1997–2003

— No survival benefit; possible harm if late

Question: Does methylprednisolone improve survival in persistent ARDS (7–28 days after onset)?

Design

  • 180 patients · ARDS for 7–28 days · PaO₂/FiO₂ < 200 at enrolment
  • Methylprednisolone: 2 mg/kg loading → 0.5 mg/kg q6h × 14 days, then taper vs placebo
  • Primary: 60-day hospital mortality
  • Sample size reduced from 400 → 180 after 2 years (slow enrolment + new data)

Key numbers

OutcomeMethylprednisolonePlacebo
60-day mortality — overall29.2%28.6% (P=1.0)
60-day mortality — enrolled >14 days after ARDS onset35%8% (P=0.02)
180-day mortality — overall31.5%31.9% (P=1.0)

Pearls

  • Early physiology improved: PaO₂/FiO₂ higher, Pplat lower, compliance better
  • More shock-free days, more ventilator-free days — but no survival benefit
  • Neuromyopathy more common with methylprednisolone (9 vs 2 patients)
  • No increase in serious secondary infections
  • Only 5% of eligible patients randomised — very selective
  • Methylprednisolone stopped abruptly 3 days post-extubation — possible rebound (Meduri 2018 re-analysis)
Later evidence: DEXA-ARDS (Villar 2020): dexamethasone 20 mg × 5 days then 10 mg × 5 days in ARDS → more ventilator-free days (12.3 vs 7.5) and lower 60-day mortality (21% vs 36%). RECOVERY (COVID-19 ARDS): dexamethasone 6 mg × 10 days reduced mortality in patients on oxygen/ventilation. Corticosteroids are now standard in COVID-19 ARDS, but routine use in non-COVID ARDS remains debated.
